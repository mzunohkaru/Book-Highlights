#### Highlights & Notes

> “What does success look like to you?”

> Success can only be based on well-defined goals. A client who cannot envision success does not have goals to pin it on. This is a red flag as goal setting is not a marketing function. They need a corporate strategist.

> “What metrics do you use?”

> Or variations thereof such as “what do you measure?” and “how do you measure?” or better yet, what parts of your data and statistics are generated via marketing automation tools and what parts are manually-sourced? I like to study this data - when available - to get an objective picture of where the prospect actually stands.

> “What is your expected timeframe for achieving X?”

> Part of our job is setting timeframe expectations. If a completely-unknown brand is asking you to build international mindshare in several months - or increase sales threefold in a month - unless you have Lady Gaga’s or Jay Z’s cell number handy - that’s not going to happen. And even if you do have the golden rolodex, the long tail on spiked initiatives are where the real work begins.

> “Tell me about your marketing staff now”

> There’s a reason a company looks for marketing talent outside its own walls. You need to understand that motivation. Is it a staff level or competency issue? Is the project too small or too insignificant to assign internal resources? What politics are at play if any? This question opens up all sorts of important discovery paths.

> “Please describe your vision to me”

> Very rare is the client who can answer this in one sentence or less. Much more common is the client with a poorly-defined or articulated vision statement. Part of the job is often to refine that story into a few words anyone can immediately understand. It’s probably safe to avoid prospects who answer “what do you mean by that?”.

> “What do your competitors do better and why?”

> Most clients have canned answers to the complementary question - namely: what do your competitors fail at? - but it’s a lot harder to properly analyze why and how others are eating your lunch. It’s also the first step to forging a path to parity and eventually surpassment - which is part of the marketing function.

> “What makes you think you’re so much better than [insert competitor]?”

> I love this confrontational question because the answer is irrelevant - what I look for here is passion. You should never work for a client who has no passion. Non-negotiable.

> “Why haven’t you done this before?”

> I like to have a clear understanding of timing in a client’s decision process. Was it a matter of resources? Shifting market needs? Strategic change? New management? Timing is very important - especially if it is off.

> “Who was doing this for you before?”

> Are you replacing internal staff or supplementing them? Was there another agency or consultant before? If so, why didn’t it work out? How long were they at it? It’s crucial to know what context you’re being brought into.

> “What makes your product (or service) unique and different?”

> If a client knows the answer to this, why hasn’t she been able to communicate it effectively before? Is the product really unique and different (it’s a marketing issue) or is this just wishful thinking (it’s a product development or innovation issue).

> “What’s the story behind your brand?”

> Is there a compelling story to amplify? If so, is content available? This opens up a whole conversation about content strategy and availability without getting too technical from the get-go.

> “What’s your typical buyer (customer) like?”

> This is one of my favorite because it’s so rich in maturity gauging. Many clients cannot answer that. Period. That alone is a sign they desperately need marketing help. Most segment markets into various “personas”, which is great, but in high-fluctuation markets (and which aren’t these days?), even those evolve quickly. More work to refine and track those in real time. Lastly, some clients will say “we don’t have a typical customer, anyone is a potential customer”. This is the best scenario from a marketing viewpoint - unless of course, they’re delusional.  

> “What is your budget for this?”

> I never ask that question because it’s a stupid question. My role is to clearly understand your needs and expectations, then tell you what you should expect to invest to achieve the goals. There is never enough budget, and there are never enough resources. The budget is what it is. Either the ROI is worth your dime, or it isn’t. The best I can do is help you make that call and adjust expectations as needed.

> First, I’m convinced you learn more by asking questions and quietly listening to answers than anything else.  Second, it can create eye-opening self-discovery moments for a prospect, and it adds value for them right from the start. Third, it lets you quickly filter out prospects who, for one reason or another, may not work out.
